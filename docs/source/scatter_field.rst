############################
Scatter field computation
############################

Near field scatter computation
===============================================

Now we repeat here the notation for the :math:`\mathcal{L}` and :math:`\mathcal{K}` operators used in the near-field scatter computation, as defined in the theory section. For computation of the scatter field we use the entire integral operator without the pricipal value combention, since we generally do not evaluate the field at points on the surface of the scatterer.

.. math::
   :label: eq:Loperator_scatter

   \mathcal{L}_{k_i}\{\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r})\}
   = \int_{S} \left[
       \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}')\,G_i
       - \frac{1}{k_i^2}\big(\nabla'_{S}\!\cdot \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}')\big)
         \nabla' G_i
     \right] \mathrm{d}S',

.. math::
   :label: eq:Koperator_scatter

   \mathcal{K}_{k_i}\{\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r})\}
   = \int_{S} \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}') \times \nabla' G_i \, \mathrm{d}S'.

.. note::

   The only difference with respect to the operators defined in the theory section is the omission of the principal value notation, since we do not evaluate the field at points on the surface of the scatterer.

We can compute the scatter field in two ways: either directly using the surface current coefficients obtained from solving the MoM system, or by first assembling a so called "near field matrix" and multiplying it by the surface current coefficients. The latter approach is more efficient when we need to evaluate the scatter field at many different points in space, since the near field matrix only needs to be assembled once.

We prefer the near field matrix approach, since in most practical applications we need to evaluate the scatter field at many points in space (e.g., for plotting radiation patterns or near-field distributions).

Near field matrix assembly
===============================================
We can substitute the RWG basis function expansion of the surface current :math:`\mathbf{X} = \sum_{i=0}^2 \alpha_{ni} \mathbf{f}_{ni}` into the integral operators defined in equations :eq:`eq:Loperator_scatter` and :eq:`eq:Koperator_scatter`. This leads to the following expressions for the scatter field at a point :math:`\mathbf{r}` in space:

.. math::
   :label: eq:E_scatter_field

   \mathbf{E}_{\text{scat}}(\mathbf{r})
   = -j\omega\mu_2 \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \alpha_{J,n} \mathcal{L}_{k_2}\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}(\mathbf{r})
     - \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \alpha_{M,n} \mathcal{K}_{k_2}\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}(\mathbf{r})

.. math::
   :label: eq:H_scatter_field

   \mathbf{H}_{\text{scat}}(\mathbf{r})
   = -j\omega\epsilon_2 \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \alpha_{M,n} \mathcal{L}_{k_2}\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}(\mathbf{r})
     + \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \alpha_{J,n} \mathcal{K}_{k_2}\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}(\mathbf{r})
     
or equivalently in matrix index notation, where we sum over all :math:`N` basis functions (edges):

.. math::
   :label: eq:scatter_field_alt
    
   \mathbf{E}_{\text{scat}}(\mathbf{r}_m)
   = -j\omega\mu_2 \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \mathcal{L}_{m,n} \alpha_{J,n}
     - \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \mathcal{K}_{m,n} \alpha_{M,n}

.. math::
   \mathbf{H}_{\text{scat}}(\mathbf{r}_m)
   = -j\omega\epsilon_2 \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \mathcal{L}_{m,n} \alpha_{M,n}
     + \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \mathcal{K}_{m,n} \alpha_{J,n}

where :math:`m` indexes the observation points, and :math:`n` indexes the global basis functions (edges) from :math:`0` to :math:`N-1`. The terms :math:`\mathcal{L}_{m,n}` and :math:`\mathcal{K}_{m,n}` represent the fields at point :math:`\mathbf{r}_m` generated by the :math:`n`-th basis function :math:`\mathbf{f}_n`:

.. math::
   :label: eq:near_field_matrices

   \mathcal{L}_{m,n} = \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}(\mathbf{r}_m), \quad
   \mathcal{K}_{m,n} = \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}(\mathbf{r}_m).

With these definitions, we can express the scatter field at all observation points as a matrix-vector product:

.. math::
   :label: eq:scatter_field_matrix

   \mathbf{E}_{\text{scat}} = -j\omega\mu_2 \mathbf{L}\mathbf{\alpha}_J - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{\alpha}_M

.. math::

   \mathbf{H}_{\text{scat}} = -j\omega\epsilon_2 \mathbf{L}\mathbf{\alpha}_M + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{\alpha}_J

The near field matrices :math:`\mathbf{L}` and :math:`\mathbf{K}` can be assembled using numerical quadrature. Recalling that each RWG basis function :math:`\mathbf{f}_n` is composed of two half-basis functions on adjacent triangles, :math:`\mathbf{f}_n = \mathbf{f}_{n_1, i_1} + \mathbf{f}_{n_2, i_2}`, the total field is a sum of contributions from each half-basis function. To align with our implementation strategy, we will compute the contributions from each face and then assemble the final result.

Let's derive the expressions for a single half-basis function :math:`\mathbf{f}_{ni}` defined on face :math:`n` and associated with its :math:`i`-th local edge.

The RWG half-basis function on a source face :math:`f_n` associated with local edge :math:`i` is given by:

.. math::

   \mathbf f_{ni}(\mathbf r') = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n}\,\big(\mathbf r'-\mathbf r_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big)

and its surface divergence is constant over the face:

.. math::

   \nabla'_S\!\cdot \mathbf f_{ni} = \frac{e_{ni}}{A_n}

Substituting these into the operator definitions for a single basis function, we get the contribution to the scattered field at an observation point :math:`\mathbf{r}` from the current on edge :math:`ni`.

For the :math:`\mathcal{L}` operator:

.. math::
   :label: eq:L_scatter_rwg

   \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{A_n} \int_{f_n} \left[
       \frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')
       - \frac{1}{k_i^2} \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')
     \right] \mathrm{d}S'

And for the :math:`\mathcal{K}` operator:

.. math::
   :label: eq:K_scatter_rwg

   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \int_{f_n}
     \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')
     \, \mathrm{d}S'

These integrals are evaluated over the source triangle :math:`f_n` for each observation point :math:`\mathbf{r}`. The next step is to apply numerical quadrature (like the Dunavant rule) to approximate these integrals.

Code-Ready Quadrature Formulas
------------------------------------
We start by converting the surface integral over the source triangle :math:`f_n` into a Dunavant quadrature sum:

.. math::
   \int_{f_n} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}') \, \mathrm{d}S' \approx A_n \sum_{q=0}^{Q-1} w_q \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}_q)

where :math:`A_n` is the area of triangle :math:`f_n`, :math:`\mathbf{r}_q` is the position of the :math:`q`-th quadrature point, and :math:`w_q` is its corresponding weight.

**1. Code-Ready L Operator**

Applying the quadrature to :eq:`eq:L_scatter_rwg`:

.. math::
   \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   \approx \frac{e_{ni}}{A_n} \left( A_n \sum_{q=0}^{Q-1} w_q \left[
       \frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbf{r}_q - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}_q)
       - \frac{1}{k_i^2} \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}_q)
     \right] \right)

The :math:`A_n` terms cancel:

.. math::
   :label: eq:L_scatter_quad

   \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   \approx e_{ni} \sum_{q=0}^{Q-1} w_q \left[\frac{1}{2}
       \big(\mathbf{r}_q - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}_q)
       - \frac{1}{k_i^2} \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}_q)
     \right]

**2. Code-Ready K Operator**

Similarly, applying the quadrature to :eq:`eq:K_scatter_rwg`:

.. math::
   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   \approx \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \left( A_n \sum_{q=0}^{Q-1} w_q
     \left[ \big(\mathbf{r}_q - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}_q) \right]
     \right)

Again, the :math:`A_n` terms cancel, yielding the final expression:

.. math::
   :label: eq:K_scatter_quad

   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   \approx e_{ni} \sum_{q=0}^{Q-1} w_q
     \left[ \frac{1}{2} \big(\mathbf{r}_q - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}_q) \right]

These two equations, :eq:`eq:L_scatter_quad` and :eq:`eq:K_scatter_quad`, are the "code-ready" formulas we need. They express the operators as a weighted sum over quadrature points, using the Green's function and its gradient, which we have already implemented.

Singularity Extraction for Near-Field Evaluation
------------------------------------------------

When evaluating the scattered field at observation points that are very close to or exactly on the surface of the scatterer (e.g., to verify boundary conditions or calculate near-field distributions), the standard numerical quadrature described above loses accuracy. This happens because the Green's function :math:`G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')` and its gradient :math:`\nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')` become singular as the distance :math:`R = \|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'\| \to 0`.

In our CUDA implementation, the standard unregularized Green's function and its gradient with respect to the source coordinates are computed by the :cpp:func:`::greenFunction3D` and :cpp:func:`::gradSourceGreenFunction` routines, respectively (or combined in :cpp:func:`::greenAndGradSourceGreenFunction`).

To make the field evaluation reliable near the surface, we must apply a singularity extraction technique similar to the one used for the MoM impedance matrix elements (see :ref:`singular`). This involves subtracting the leading singular terms from the integrands, evaluating the remaining "regularized" integrals using numerical quadrature, and then adding back the exact analytical integrals of the extracted singular terms.

Step 1 — Starting equations
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The fields generated by a single half-basis function :math:`\mathbf{f}_{ni}` on face :math:`f_n` are evaluated at an observation point :math:`\mathbf{r}`. From the operator definitions, we have:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{A_n} \int_{f_n} \left[
       \frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')
       - \frac{1}{k_i^2} \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')
     \right] \mathrm{d}S'

.. math::

   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \int_{f_n}
     \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')
     \, \mathrm{d}S'

Substituting the explicit form of the Green's function gradient :math:`\nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') = \big(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\big) G_i \hat{\mathbf{R}}`, with :math:`\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'`, :math:`R = \|\mathbf{R}\|`, and :math:`\hat{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R}/R`, yields:

.. math::

   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \int_{f_n} \left(jk_i+\frac{1}{R}\right) G_i \Big[ \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \hat{\mathbf{R}} \Big] \, \mathrm{d}S'

Following Hodges and Rahmat-Samii (1997) :cite:p:`hodges1997mfie`, the cross-product term in the integrand is undesirable because it complicates evaluation. To eliminate it, we first decompose the distance vector :math:`\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'`. We define a constant vector with respect to the integration variables, :math:`\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}`. The vector :math:`\mathbf{R}` can then be decomposed as:

.. math::

   \mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}) - (\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}) = \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} - \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big)

Using this decomposition, the cross product term becomes:

.. math::

   \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \hat{\mathbf{R}} 
   = \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \frac{\mathbf{R}}{R}
   = \frac{1}{R} \Big[ \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \big(\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} - (\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}})\big) \Big]

Since the cross product of a vector with itself is zero, this simplifies to:

.. math::

   \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \hat{\mathbf{R}} 
   = \frac{1}{R} \Big[ \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \times \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \Big]
   = -\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \times \frac{\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}}{R}

Because :math:`\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}}` is independent of the integration variable :math:`\mathbf{r}'`, we can factor the cross product outside the integral. The :math:`\mathcal{K}` operator becomes:

.. math::

   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \times \int_{f_n} \left(jk_i+\frac{1}{R}\right) G_i \frac{\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}}{R} \, \mathrm{d}S'


Evaluation of the :math:`\mathcal{L}` Operator
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
To evaluate the singular contributions, we separate the :math:`\mathcal{L}` operator into two integrals:

.. math::
   
   \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \mathcal{L}^{(1)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) + \mathcal{L}^{(2)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})

where the first integral involves the basis function vector, and the second involves the gradient of the Green's function:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(1)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \int_{f_n} \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') \, \mathrm{d}S'

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(2)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \int_{f_n} \nabla' G_i(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') \, \mathrm{d}S'

First Integral
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
Following the same procedure as in the :ref:`theory section <sie_L_singularity_extraction>`, we isolate the singularity by splitting the Green's function into regular and singular (Coulomb) parts:

.. math::

   G_i = G_{\mathrm{reg}} + \frac{1}{4\pi R}

Substituting this decomposition into the first integral yields a regular part (which can be evaluated using standard Dunavant quadrature) and a singular part:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{f_n} \frac{\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}}{R} \, \mathrm{d}S'
   

As established in the :ref:`theory section <sie_L_singularity_extraction>`, this integral corresponds exactly to the :math:`\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r})` potential. By rewriting the numerator as :math:`(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}) + (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}})`, the integral separates into the standard scalar and vector potentials:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \Big[ \boldsymbol{\phi}_1(\mathbf{r}) + \big(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \phi_0(\mathbf{r}) \Big] = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r})

The regular part of the first integral is evaluated numerically using standard Dunavant quadrature:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{reg}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \int_{f_n} \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) G_{\mathrm{reg}}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') \, \mathrm{d}S'

where the regularized Green's function is :math:`G_{\mathrm{reg}} = \frac{e^{-jk_i R} - 1}{4\pi R}`. In the codebase, this is implemented by :cpp:func:`::greenFunction3DRegular` (and jointly with the gradient in :cpp:func:`::greenAndGradSourceGreenFunctionRegular`). To prevent numerical instability (catastrophic cancellation) when evaluated near the singularity, the implementation substitutes its limiting value for small :math:`R`:

.. math::

   \lim_{R \to 0} G_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) = -\frac{jk_i}{4\pi}

The total evaluation of the first integral is simply the sum of these numerical and analytical parts:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(1)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{reg}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) + \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})

Second Integral
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
For the second integral, we begin by substituting the exact formula for the gradient of the Green's function, :math:`\nabla' G_i = \big(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\big) G_i \hat{\mathbf{R}}`, directly into the equation:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(2)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \int_{f_n} \left(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\right) G_i \hat{\mathbf{R}} \, \mathrm{d}S'

To facilitate the evaluation, we rewrite the unit vector as :math:`\hat{\mathbf{R}} = \frac{\mathbf{R}}{R}`. Recalling the decomposition of the distance vector from the :math:`\mathcal{K}` operator derivation, we have:

.. math::

   \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} - \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big)

where :math:`\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}` is a constant vector relative to the integration variables. Substituting this into the integrand and distributing the terms allows us to split :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)}` into two separate integrals:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(2)}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = &-\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \int_{f_n} \left(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\right) \frac{G_i}{R} \, \mathrm{d}S' \\
     &+\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \int_{f_n} \left(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\right) \frac{G_i}{R} \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \, \mathrm{d}S'

Recognizing that the scalar term inside the integrals is exactly the magnitude of the gradient, we can define the kernel function :math:`K(R) = 4\pi \left(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\right) \frac{G_i}{R}`. 

Following the same approach as the :math:`\mathcal{K}` operator (see :eq:`eq:TK_K_reg_func`), we separate this kernel into its regular and singular parts, :math:`K(R) = K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) + K_{\mathrm{sing}}(R)`, where:

.. math::

   K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) &= \left( \frac{jk_i}{R^2} + \frac{1}{R^3} \right) e^{-jk_i R} - \left(\frac{1}{R^3} + \frac{k_i^2}{2R}\right) \\
   K_{\mathrm{sing}}(R) &= \frac{1}{R^3} + \frac{k_i^2}{2R}

Substituting this split back into the equations allows us to cleanly separate :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)}` into its regular and singular components: :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)} = \mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{reg}} + \mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sing}}`.

The regular part :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{reg}}` is evaluated numerically. To prevent numerical instability near the singularity, its limiting value :math:`\lim_{R \to 0} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) = -jk_i^3/3` is used:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{reg}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = &-\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \int_{f_n} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) \, \mathrm{d}S' \\
     &+\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{f_n} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \, \mathrm{d}S'

The singular part :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sing}}` contains the analytical singularities that must be integrated:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = &-\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \int_{f_n} \left(\frac{1}{R^3} + \frac{k_i^2}{2R}\right) \, \mathrm{d}S' \\
     &+\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{f_n} \left(\frac{1}{R^3} + \frac{k_i^2}{2R}\right) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \, \mathrm{d}S'

We can evaluate these integrals directly using the standard scalar and vector potentials defined in the :ref:`singular term handling <singular>` section:

1. :math:`\int_{f_n} \frac{1}{R} \, \mathrm{d}S' = \phi_0(\mathbf{r})`
2. :math:`\int_{f_n} \frac{1}{R^3} \, \mathrm{d}S' = \phi_2(\mathbf{r})`
3. :math:`\int_{f_n} \frac{\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}}{R} \, \mathrm{d}S' = \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r})`

4. :math:`\int_{f_n} \frac{\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}}{R^3} \, \mathrm{d}S' = \boldsymbol{\phi}_3(\mathbf{r})`

Substituting these four evaluated integrals back into the equation yields the analytical evaluation for :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sing}}`:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = &-\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \Big( \phi_2(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{k_i^2}{2}\phi_0(\mathbf{r}) \Big) \\
     &+\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \Big( \boldsymbol{\phi}_3(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{k_i^2}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r}) \Big)

Total Evaluation of the :math:`\mathcal{K}` Operator
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Recognizing that the scalar term inside the integrand is exactly the magnitude of the gradient, we can define the kernel function :math:`K(R) = 4\pi \left(jk_i + \frac{1}{R}\right) \frac{G_i}{R}`. The operator can then be written compactly as:

.. math::

   \mathcal{K}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \times \int_{f_n} K(R) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \, \mathrm{d}S'

Following the exact same approach as used throughout the :ref:`singular term handling <singular>` section, we split this kernel into its regular and singular parts, :math:`K(R) = K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) + K_{\mathrm{sing}}(R)`. This perfectly separates the operator into its regular and singular components: :math:`\mathcal{K} = \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{reg}} + \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}`.

The **total regular term** is evaluated numerically via quadrature:

.. math::

   \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{reg}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \times \int_{f_n} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \, \mathrm{d}S'

The **total singular term** contains the analytical singularities:

.. math::

   \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \times \int_{f_n} \left( \frac{1}{R^3} + \frac{k_i^2}{2R} \right) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big) \, \mathrm{d}S'

Notice that this singular integral corresponds exactly to the inner Coulomb integral :math:`\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}^{(\mathrm{coul})}(\mathbf{r})` derived in the :ref:`singular term handling <singular>` section (see :eq:`eq:TK_coul_part`). Substituting the standard fundamental vector potentials :math:`\boldsymbol{\phi}_3(\mathbf{r})` and :math:`\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r})` yields the direct analytical evaluation:

.. math::
   :label: eq:K_scatter_sing

   \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \boldsymbol{\Upsilon}^{(\mathrm{coul})}(\mathbf{r})
   = -\frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \times \Big( \boldsymbol{\phi}_3(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{k_i^2}{2} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r}) \Big)


Total Evaluation of the :math:`\mathcal{L}` Operator
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

We can now cleanly write the complete evaluation of the scattered field :math:`\mathcal{L}` operator by combining the regular and singular components of both integrals:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})

The **total regular term** is the sum of the regularized integrals from both terms, evaluated numerically using Dunavant quadrature:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{e_{ni}}{A_n} \int_{f_n} \left[ 
       \frac{1}{2} G_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big)
       - \frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}}}{4\pi k_i^2} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) 
       + \frac{1}{4\pi k_i^2} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(R) \big(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_{\overline{\partial e_{ni}}}\big)
   \right] \, \mathrm{d}S'

The **total singular term** is obtained by directly summing our analytical evaluations for :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sing}}` and :math:`\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sing}}`:

.. math::

   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r}) 
   = &\frac{e_{ni}}{2A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r}) \\
     &-\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \Big( \phi_2(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{k_i^2}{2}\phi_0(\mathbf{r}) \Big) \\
     &+\frac{e_{ni}}{A_n k_i^2} \frac{1}{4\pi} \Big( \boldsymbol{\phi}_3(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{k_i^2}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r}) \Big)

Grouping the terms with the fundamental potentials, we note that the two :math:`\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1` terms perfectly sum up: :math:`\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1 + \frac{1}{k_i^2}\frac{k_i^2}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1 = \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1`. Rearranging the remaining terms yields the final analytical expression in terms of only the four core potentials:

.. math::
   :label: eq:L_scatter_sing

   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sing}}\{\mathbf{f}_{ni}\}(\mathbf{r})
   = \frac{e_{ni}}{A_n} \frac{1}{4\pi} \left[ \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1(\mathbf{r}) - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}}\phi_0(\mathbf{r}) + \frac{1}{k_i^2} \Big( \boldsymbol{\phi}_3(\mathbf{r}) - \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{free}} \phi_2(\mathbf{r}) \Big) \right]

This provides a complete, code-ready analytical correction for the scattered field evaluation near the source surface, avoiding the need for an outer testing quadrature and fully bypassing the explicit intermediate calculation of :math:`\nabla \phi_0`.
